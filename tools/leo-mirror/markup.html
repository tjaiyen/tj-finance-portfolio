Why a realised rate, not factory capacity. Capacity tells you what the line could do; it has never been the binding constraint here. Using it would produce a projection that clears the deadline comfortably and would be wrong for the same reason a plan built on best-case supply is wrong.

Why the FCC condition and not the full constellation. The full authorisation matters commercially; the 50% condition is the one with a dated legal consequence attached. Finance should be governing to the date that carries a penalty.

Requirement, deadline, consequence and waiver status are all from the FCC grant document. The projection is mine.

Total cost of ownership is unit price plus everything the unit price hides: tooling, qualification, yield loss, carrying cost, and the cost of switching if the supplier fails. A make-vs-buy decided on unit price alone reliably picks the option that looks cheapest in year one and is most expensive by year three.

In this programme's context the interesting TCO question is not a component at all — it is launch. Recommendation 3 in Chapter 4 argues that the payback on a fourth launch provider is a TCO question finance should already have modelled.

Price — you bought the same thing and paid a different rate. Usually a supplier or FX story; the fix lives in procurement.

Actual cost already exceeds the full budget. That makes TCPI-to-BAC mathematically negative ({{ evm.tcpiB }}) — there is no efficiency that recovers the original budget, because the money is already gone. TCPI-to-EAC ({{ evm.tcpiE }}) is the only recovery target left, and it is reported rather than quietly suppressed.
CPI = EV ÷ AC
SPI = EV ÷ PV (PV = BAC at this reporting point)
CV = EV − AC
SV = EV − PV
EAC_typical = BAC + (AC − EV)
EAC_cpi = BAC ÷ CPI
EAC_composite = AC + (BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI)
VAC = BAC − EAC_cpi
TCPI = (BAC − EV) ÷ (target − AC)

Why three EACs and not one. Each embeds a different belief about the rest of the programme: that the overrun was a one-off, that it repeats at the same cost efficiency, or that both cost and schedule inefficiency persist. Publishing one number would be publishing a belief without labelling it.

Glossary

Every term this page uses, in plain language. Nothing here assumes you have run a programme P&L before.

BAC — Budget at Completion

What you said the whole thing would cost. The number in the approved plan.

AC — Actual Cost

Money genuinely spent to date. No judgement attached about whether it bought anything.

EV — Earned Value

The budgeted value of work actually finished. The bridge between spend and progress, and the number most reports quietly skip.

PV — Planned Value

What you should have finished by now according to the schedule.

CPI — Cost Performance Index

EV ÷ AC. Below 1.0 means every dollar bought less than a dollar of progress. At 0.86 you are getting 86 cents of work per dollar spent.

SPI — Schedule Performance Index

EV ÷ PV. Below 1.0 means behind the planned curve. It says nothing at all about cost.

CV / SV — Cost & Schedule Variance

The same two ideas expressed in dollars rather than ratios. Negative is bad in both.

EAC — Estimate at Completion

What it will now cost given what has happened so far. Three versions exist because each embeds a different belief about whether the overrun repeats.

VAC — Variance at Completion

BAC minus EAC. How far over or under you now expect to finish.

TCPI — To-Complete Performance Index

The efficiency you would have to hit from here to land on a target. If it comes out negative, the target is already unreachable.

OP1 / OP2

Amazon's annual planning cycle — OP1 in the autumn, OP2 as the January reset. The documents a Finance Manager is measured on.

CapEx vs. OpEx

CapEx buys things that last and is spread over years. OpEx is the cost of running this year. Moving spend between them changes reported profit without changing cash.

TCO — Total Cost of Ownership

Unit price plus everything unit price hides: tooling, qualification, yield loss, carrying cost, switching cost.

Payback & ROI

Payback is how long until an investment returns its cost. ROI is the annual return as a percentage of it. Neither prices risk on its own.

Variance drivers

Price (paid a different rate), volume (bought a different quantity), scope (bought something unplanned), timing (right money, wrong period). Only timing reverses on its own.

Pareto analysis

Ranking causes so you can see which handful explain most of the effect — the vital few against the long tail.

Capitalised inventory

Finished goods carried on the balance sheet at cost rather than expensed. Fine while they are worth what you paid; a write-down when they are not.

Priority status (FCC)

First claim on an orbit and a frequency. Losing it is not losing the licence — it is losing your place in the queue, which is close to the same thing commercially.

OISL — Optical Inter-Satellite Link

Laser terminals that let satellites talk to each other instead of routing everything via the ground. Few suppliers make them well.

D2D / SCS

Direct-to-device: satellites talking straight to ordinary phones. SCS is the FCC framework that regulates it.

Exposure tier

Probability crossed with impact, expressed as a band rather than a false-precision number. A tier admits what a percentage pretends to know.
